Platform Economics: Coordination Costs in Digital Markets

Abstract

We study how digital platforms reduce coordination costs between buyers and sellers. Using a dataset of 1,240 transactions across three marketplaces, we show that platform-mediated transactions have 38% lower search costs than off-platform transactions. We argue that the main mechanism is standardization of listings rather than algorithmic matching. Our results are robust to controls for product category and seller experience.

1. Introduction

Digital platforms are often described as matching engines. This framing emphasizes algorithms and recommendation systems. We argue that the more important function of platforms is standardization: by forcing heterogeneous goods into comparable listings, platforms make search cheaper for everyone.

This paper asks a narrow question: how much of the observed reduction in coordination costs is attributable to standardization rather than matching?

2. Related Work

The literature on two-sided markets emphasizes cross-side network effects (Rochet and Tirole, 2003). A separate literature studies search frictions in online markets (Baye et al., 2004). We connect these two strands by treating listing standardization as a supply-side investment that reduces search frictions.

3. Data and Method

We collected transaction-level data from three marketplaces between 2019 and 2022. The sample contains 1,240 transactions and 318 sellers. Search cost is measured as the elapsed time between the first query and the completed purchase.

We exploit a natural experiment: one marketplace introduced a standardized listing template in 2021. We compare transactions before and after the change, using the other two marketplaces as controls.

4. Results

Standardized listings reduce search time by 38% (p < 0.01). The effect is concentrated among inexperienced buyers, for whom search time falls by 52%. For experienced buyers the effect is only 11% and not statistically significant.

Algorithmic matching, by contrast, explains at most 9% of the variance in search time. This is consistent with our hypothesis that standardization dominates matching.

5. Discussion

Our results suggest that platform designers should invest in listing standards before investing in recommendation algorithms. This is a counterintuitive implication, because matching algorithms are more visible and easier to demo.

The study has limitations. All three marketplaces sell consumer electronics, so external validity is limited. Search time is also an imperfect proxy for coordination costs.

6. Conclusion

Platforms reduce coordination costs mainly by standardizing what is being exchanged, not by matching better. This finding reframes the platform as an information infrastructure rather than a matching engine.
